----------------------------------------------------

  strands: Analysis (11.1-11.3), Number theory and arithmetic geometry
  (11.4, 11.5, 11.10), Geometry, algebra and topology (11.6-11.9, 11.11,
  11.17), Applied and discrete mathematics (11.12-11.16) and the Research
  project (11.18).
  Each topic has:
    * WHAT THE CHILD MUST LEARN   the checklist of definitions, theorems,
                                  standard examples, counterexamples and
                                  methods in that topic
    * PART A — DESCRIPTIVE        12 questions (D1-D12): proofs,
                                  explanations, derivations and worked
                                  numerical problems, each with the answer
                                  and "Teacher explains" (the method step
                                  by step as a teacher shows it on the
                                  board, common mistakes, and a "Now you
                                  try" question)
    * PART B — MATHEMATICAL ONLY  12 questions (M1-M12): pure drills with
                                  answers. Most have many parts (a, b,
                                  c ...) so that EVERY case of the topic is
                                  practised again and again, from the
                                  easiest to the hardest — many measures
                                  and integrals, many norms and operators,
                                  many number fields and curves, many
                                  groups, rings and characters, many
                                  chains, maps and graphs, many codes and
                                  data sets — not just one example of each
                                  idea.
    * TEACHING TIPS               what to do if the student struggles, and
                                  how to make it harder
- In total: 216 descriptive + 216 mathematical questions, then a mixed
  revision test (equal descriptive and mathematical parts, two questions
  from every topic in each part) and a readiness checklist for Stage 12.
- Level: final-year honours mathematics: Measure and Integration, Lp
  spaces, Functional Analysis, Algebraic and Analytic Number Theory,
  Manifolds, Lie Groups and Lie Algebras, Commutative Algebra,
  Representation Theory, Elliptic Curves, Riemann Surfaces, Stochastic

  Differential Topology, and the Research Project.
- What is new in Stage 11: DEPTH AND CHOICE. The student meets the
  modern forms of the subjects of Stage 10 — the Lebesgue integral that
  makes limits and integrals commute, spaces of functions treated as
  geometric spaces, primes studied through rings and through complex
  analysis, curved spaces that are locally Euclidean, groups studied
  through matrices, randomness and chaos as mathematical objects — and
  begins to read and write mathematics like a researcher.
- Conventions: N = {1, 2, 3, ...} unless 0 is stated; log means ln unless

  measure; a.e. = almost everywhere; ‖·‖ is a norm; ⟨·,·⟩ an inner
  product (linear in the first slot); Zₙ = Z/nZ; Fₚ is the field with p

  Use Python/SageMath/R to CHECK calculations and to explore, and write

- Stage 11 is a menu of electives: a typical honours student takes 6-10
  of them in depth and meets the others at the level of the checklist and
  Part B. Do 2-3 hours a day: 2 descriptive questions (write the full
  proof or solution) and 2 drills per session, plus weekly mixed revision.
  Topic 11.18 (the project) runs alongside everything else all year.
- Revise Stage 10 (A10.txt) whenever the student finds something

TOPIC 11.1 — MEASURE THEORY AND LEBESGUE INTEGRATION: SIGMA-ALGEBRAS,
LEBESGUE MEASURE, MEASURABLE FUNCTIONS, MONOTONE AND DOMINATED CONVERGENCE,
FUBINI
================================================================================
WHAT THE CHILD MUST LEARN
  - σ-ALGEBRA on X: a family 𝒜 of subsets with X ∈ 𝒜, closed under
    complements and COUNTABLE unions (hence countable intersections,
    differences, lim sup and lim inf of sets). (X, 𝒜) is a MEASURABLE
    SPACE. The σ-algebra GENERATED by a family 𝒞, σ(𝒞), is the
    intersection of all σ-algebras containing 𝒞. BOREL sets 𝔅(R) =
    σ(open sets) = σ(intervals (a, b]) = σ(rays (−∞, a]).
  - MEASURE: μ : 𝒜 → [0, ∞] with μ(∅) = 0 and COUNTABLE ADDITIVITY
    μ(⋃Aₙ) = ∑μ(Aₙ) for disjoint Aₙ. Consequences: monotone, countably
    SUBadditive, CONTINUITY FROM BELOW (Aₙ ↑ A ⇒ μ(Aₙ) ↑ μ(A)) and FROM
    ABOVE (Aₙ ↓ A and μ(A₁) < ∞ ⇒ μ(Aₙ) ↓ μ(A); fails without μ(A₁) < ∞:
    Aₙ = [n, ∞)). Examples: counting measure, Dirac measure δₓ, Lebesgue
    measure, probability measures.
  - LEBESGUE OUTER MEASURE m*(E) = inf{∑ ℓ(Iₖ) : E ⊆ ⋃Iₖ, Iₖ open
    intervals}; m*(interval) = length; m* is translation invariant and
    countably subadditive; countable sets have measure 0. CARATHÉODORY:
    E is measurable iff m*(A) = m*(A ∩ E) + m*(A ∖ E) for every A; the
    measurable sets form a σ-algebra ℒ ⊇ 𝔅 on which m is a complete
    measure. REGULARITY: m(E) = inf{m(U) : U ⊇ E open} = sup{m(K) : K ⊆ E
    compact}. The VITALI set shows not every subset of R is measurable.
  - NULL SETS AND THE CANTOR SET: Q, any countable set, and the Cantor set C
    (uncountable!) have measure 0; fat Cantor sets are nowhere dense with
    positive measure. "Almost everywhere" (a.e.) = outside a null set.
  - MEASURABLE FUNCTIONS: f : X → [−∞, ∞] with {f > a} ∈ 𝒜 for every a
    (equivalently f⁻¹(B) ∈ 𝒜 for Borel B). Continuous and monotone
    functions are Borel measurable; sums, products, max, min, sup, inf,
    lim sup, lim inf and pointwise limits of measurable functions are
    measurable. SIMPLE functions ∑ aₖ𝟙_{Aₖ}; every f ≥ 0 measurable is the
    increasing limit of simple functions φₙ = min(n, 2⁻ⁿ⌊2ⁿf⌋).
  - LEBESGUE INTEGRAL: ∫∑aₖ𝟙_{Aₖ} = ∑aₖμ(Aₖ) for simple φ ≥ 0; ∫f =
    sup{∫φ : 0 ≤ φ ≤ f simple} for f ≥ 0; f is INTEGRABLE (f ∈ L¹) if
    ∫|f| < ∞, and then ∫f = ∫f⁺ − ∫f⁻. Linear, monotone, |∫f| ≤ ∫|f|,
    ∫|f| = 0 ⇔ f = 0 a.e. MARKOV/CHEBYSHEV: μ{|f| ≥ t} ≤ (1/t)∫|f|.
  - RIEMANN VS LEBESGUE: a bounded f on [a, b] is Riemann integrable iff it
    is continuous a.e. (Lebesgue's criterion), and then the two integrals
    agree. The Dirichlet function 𝟙_Q is not Riemann integrable but ∫𝟙_Q
    dm = 0. Absolutely convergent improper Riemann integrals are Lebesgue
    integrals; ∫₀^∞ (sin x)/x dx converges only improperly (not in L¹).
  - MONOTONE CONVERGENCE THEOREM (MCT): 0 ≤ f₁ ≤ f₂ ≤ ... measurable, fₙ →
    f ⇒ ∫fₙ → ∫f. Corollary: ∫∑gₙ = ∑∫gₙ for gₙ ≥ 0. FATOU'S LEMMA: ∫lim
    inf fₙ ≤ lim inf ∫fₙ for fₙ ≥ 0 (strict for moving bumps).
  - DOMINATED CONVERGENCE THEOREM (DCT): fₙ → f a.e. and |fₙ| ≤ g with g ∈
    L¹ ⇒ ∫|fₙ − f| → 0 and ∫fₙ → ∫f. Standard failures without a
    dominating g: n𝟙_(0,1/n), 𝟙_[n,n+1], (1/n)𝟙_[0,n]. Uses: limits under
    the integral, continuity and DIFFERENTIATION UNDER THE INTEGRAL SIGN
    (|∂f/∂t| ≤ g ∈ L¹).
  - MODES OF CONVERGENCE: a.e., in measure, in L¹. L¹ ⇒ in measure ⇒ a
    subsequence a.e.; on a finite measure space a.e. ⇒ in measure
    (EGOROFF: a.e. ⇒ uniform off a set of small measure). The "typewriter"
    sequence converges in L¹ but nowhere pointwise.
  - PRODUCT MEASURE AND FUBINI-TONELLI: on σ-finite spaces there is a unique
    μ × ν with (μ × ν)(A × B) = μ(A)ν(B). TONELLI: for f ≥ 0 measurable the
    iterated integrals (in either order) equal ∬ f d(μ × ν). FUBINI: the
    same for f ∈ L¹(μ × ν). Check ∬|f| < ∞ first (by Tonelli), or the
    orders may disagree: f = (x² − y²)/(x² + y²)² on (0, 1)² gives ±π/4.
  - LEBESGUE-STIELTJES MEASURES AND DENSITIES: an increasing right-
    continuous F gives μ_F(a, b] = F(b) − F(a). If g ≥ 0 then ν(A) = ∫_A g
    dμ is a measure with ∫f dν = ∫fg dμ (Radon-Nikodym in reverse); change
    of variables ∫_{T(A)} f = ∫_A (f ∘ T)|det DT|.
  - CONTINUITY OF THE INTEGRAL: if f ∈ L¹ then for every ε > 0 there is δ >
    0 with μ(A) < δ ⇒ ∫_A |f| < ε; F(x) = ∫₀ˣ f is (absolutely) continuous,
    and F′ = f a.e. (Lebesgue differentiation theorem).

PART A — DESCRIPTIVE
--------------------
D1. Let X = {1, 2, 3, 4}. (a) Find σ(𝒞) for 𝒞 = {{1}, {2}} and list its
    elements. (b) Is the family {∅, X, {1}, {2, 3, 4}, {1, 2}, {3, 4}} a
    σ-algebra? (c) Prove that an intersection of σ-algebras is a
    σ-algebra but a union need not be.
    Answer: (a) 8 sets: generated by the partition {1}, {2}, {3, 4}.
    (b) No. (c) Proof below; union counterexample on X = {1, 2, 3}.
    Teacher explains: A σ-algebra on a FINITE set is always generated by a
    partition into "atoms": every set in it is a union of atoms, so it has
    2^(number of atoms) elements. Here the atoms are the smallest non-empty
    sets we can make from {1}, {2} and complements: {1}, {2}, and X ∖ {1, 2}
    = {3, 4}. So σ(𝒞) consists of all unions of these three atoms:
       ∅, {1}, {2}, {3,4}, {1,2}, {1,3,4}, {2,3,4}, X     (2³ = 8 sets).
    (b) It contains {1} and {1, 2} but not {1}ᶜ ∩ {1, 2} = {2}; indeed
    {2} = {1, 2} ∩ {2, 3, 4} must lie in any σ-algebra containing both, so
    the family is not closed under intersections. (c) If each 𝒜ᵢ contains
    X and is closed under complements and countable unions, so is
    ⋂𝒜ᵢ: e.g. if Aₙ ∈ ⋂𝒜ᵢ then Aₙ ∈ 𝒜ᵢ for every i, so ⋃Aₙ ∈ 𝒜ᵢ for
    every i. Union: on {1, 2, 3}, 𝒜₁ = {∅, X, {1}, {2, 3}} and
    𝒜₂ = {∅, X, {2}, {1, 3}}; 𝒜₁ ∪ 𝒜₂ contains {1} and {2} but not
    {1, 2}. Common mistakes: forgetting ∅ and X; listing only the given
    sets and their complements (you must also take unions). Now you try:
    how many sets are in σ({1, 2}, {2, 3}) on {1, 2, 3, 4}? (atoms {1}, {2},
    {3}, {4}: 16)

D2. Prove, from countable additivity, (a) monotonicity, (b) countable
    subadditivity, (c) continuity from below and (d) continuity from above
    when μ(A₁) < ∞. Show (d) fails for Aₙ = [n, ∞) in R.
    Answer: proofs by "disjointification"; m([n, ∞)) = ∞ for all n but
    ⋂[n, ∞) = ∅.
    Teacher explains: (a) If A ⊆ B, B = A ∪ (B ∖ A) disjointly, so μ(B) =
    μ(A) + μ(B ∖ A) ≥ μ(A). (b) Given A₁, A₂, ..., put B₁ = A₁ and Bₙ = Aₙ
    ∖ (A₁ ∪ ... ∪ Aₙ₋₁). The Bₙ are disjoint, Bₙ ⊆ Aₙ and ⋃Bₙ = ⋃Aₙ, so
    μ(⋃Aₙ) = ∑μ(Bₙ) ≤ ∑μ(Aₙ). (c) If A₁ ⊆ A₂ ⊆ ..., the rings Bₙ =
    Aₙ ∖ Aₙ₋₁ (A₀ = ∅) are disjoint with A_N = B₁ ∪ ... ∪ B_N, so
       μ(⋃Aₙ) = ∑_{n=1}^{∞} μ(Bₙ) = lim_N ∑_{n≤N} μ(Bₙ) = lim_N μ(A_N).
    (d) If A₁ ⊇ A₂ ⊇ ... and μ(A₁) < ∞, apply (c) to A₁ ∖ Aₙ ↑ A₁ ∖ A:
    μ(A₁) − μ(Aₙ) → μ(A₁) − μ(A); subtraction is legal because everything is
    finite. Counterexample: m([n, ∞)) = ∞ for all n, but the intersection
    is ∅ of measure 0. Common mistakes: writing μ(B ∖ A) = μ(B) − μ(A)
    when μ(A) = ∞ (∞ − ∞ is meaningless). Now you try: in counting measure
    on N, find decreasing sets with infinite measure and empty
    intersection. ({n, n + 1, ...})

D3. (a) Show that every countable set E ⊆ R has Lebesgue measure 0.
    (b) Construct the Cantor set C, show m(C) = 0 and that C is
    uncountable. (c) Build a "fat" Cantor set of measure 1/2.
    Answer: (a) cover the k-th point by an interval of length ε/2ᵏ.
    (b) removed length 1, C ↔ ternary expansions with digits 0, 2.
    (c) at step n remove 2ⁿ⁻¹ middle intervals of length 1/4ⁿ.
    Teacher explains: (a) Let E = {x₁, x₂, ...} and ε > 0. Iₖ = (xₖ −
    ε/2ᵏ⁺¹, xₖ + ε/2ᵏ⁺¹) has length ε/2ᵏ, and the Iₖ cover E, so m*(E) ≤
    ∑ε/2ᵏ = ε. As ε is arbitrary, m(E) = 0. In particular m(Q) = 0 and
    m([0, 1] ∖ Q) = 1. (b) C₀ = [0, 1]; Cₙ₊₁ removes the open middle third
    of each of the 2ⁿ intervals of Cₙ (each of length 3⁻ⁿ); C = ⋂Cₙ.
    m(Cₙ) = (2/3)ⁿ → 0, so m(C) = 0 by continuity from above. Equivalently
    the removed length is 1/3 + 2/9 + 4/27 + ... = (1/3)/(1 − 2/3) = 1.
    C is exactly the set of x with a ternary expansion using only digits 0
    and 2; the map 0.d₁d₂d₃...(base 3) ↦ 0.(d₁/2)(d₂/2)...(base 2) sends C
    onto [0, 1], so C is uncountable. (c) At step n remove from the middle
    of each of the 2ⁿ⁻¹ remaining intervals an open interval of length
    4⁻ⁿ. The total removed is ∑_{n≥1} 2ⁿ⁻¹4⁻ⁿ = (1/4)∑(1/2)ⁿ⁻¹ = 1/2, so the
    set left is closed, nowhere dense and has measure 1/2. Common mistakes:
    "measure zero means countable" (C is a counterexample); "nowhere dense
    means measure zero" (the fat Cantor set). Now you try: remove middle
    fifths instead of thirds: the measure of what remains? (0: (4/5)ⁿ → 0)

D4. Explain the construction of a non-measurable (Vitali) set and why no
    translation-invariant, countably additive measure can measure every
    subset of R with m([0, 1]) = 1.
    Answer: choose one point from each coset of Q in [0, 1]; its rational
    translates would give 1 ≤ ∑m(V) ≤ 3, impossible.
    Teacher explains: Declare x ~ y if x − y ∈ Q. Each class meets [0, 1];
    by the axiom of choice pick one point from each class, forming V ⊆
    [0, 1]. Enumerate the rationals q₁, q₂, ... of [−1, 1] and put Vₖ =
    V + qₖ. (i) The Vₖ are DISJOINT: if v + qₖ = w + qⱼ then v − w ∈ Q, so v
    = w (one point per class) and qₖ = qⱼ. (ii) [0, 1] ⊆ ⋃Vₖ ⊆ [−1, 2]:
    every x ∈ [0, 1] is equivalent to some v ∈ V with x − v ∈ [−1, 1] ∩ Q.
    If V were measurable, translation invariance gives m(Vₖ) = m(V), and
    countable additivity gives
       1 ≤ ∑_{k=1}^{∞} m(V) ≤ 3.
    If m(V) = 0 the sum is 0 < 1; if m(V) > 0 the sum is ∞ > 3. Both are
    contradictions, so V is not measurable. This is why measure theory
    works with a σ-algebra rather than all subsets. Common mistakes:
    thinking V is "small" or "large" — it has outer measure > 0 but no
    measure; forgetting that the proof needs choice (Solovay: without it,
    all sets can be measurable). Now you try: why can we not use all reals
    q (not just rationals) in the argument? (then the union is uncountable
    and additivity says nothing)

D5. Prove that the sum and the product of two measurable functions f, g :
    X → R are measurable, and that sup fₙ and lim sup fₙ are measurable.
    Answer: {f + g > a} = ⋃_{r∈Q} ({f > r} ∩ {g > a − r}); fg = ((f + g)² −
    (f − g)²)/4; {sup fₙ > a} = ⋃{fₙ > a}.
    Teacher explains: f + g > a ⇔ f > a − g ⇔ there is a rational r with f
    > r > a − g (density of Q). So
       {f + g > a} = ⋃_{r ∈ Q} ({f > r} ∩ {g > a − r}),
    a COUNTABLE union of measurable sets. For squares: {f² > a} = X if a <
    0 and {f > √a} ∪ {f < −√a} if a ≥ 0; −f and cf are clearly
    measurable, so fg = ((f + g)² − (f − g)²)/4 is measurable. For
    suprema: sup fₙ(x) > a ⇔ some fₙ(x) > a, so {sup fₙ > a} = ⋃ₙ{fₙ >
    a}. Similarly inf fₙ = −sup(−fₙ), and lim sup fₙ = inf_k sup_{n≥k} fₙ,
    so it is measurable; lim fₙ, where it exists, equals lim sup fₙ. The
    key point: everything uses only COUNTABLE operations. Common mistakes:
    writing {f + g > a} = {f > a/2} ∪ {g > a/2} (false: f = 3, g = −1,
    a = 1); taking a supremum over uncountably many functions (not
    measurable in general). Now you try: show max(f, g) is measurable.
    ({max > a} = {f > a} ∪ {g > a})

D6. State the monotone convergence theorem. Use it to (a) show that
    ∫₀¹ (−ln x)/(1 − x) dx = ∑_{n≥1} ∫₀¹ xⁿ⁻¹(−ln x) dx and evaluate it;
    (b) justify ∫₀^∞ x/(eˣ − 1) dx = π²/6.
    Answer: both equal ∑1/n² = π²/6 ≈ 1.64493.
    Teacher explains: MCT: if 0 ≤ f₁ ≤ f₂ ≤ ... and fₙ → f pointwise, then
    ∫fₙ → ∫f (the value ∞ is allowed). Its corollary for series with
    NON-NEGATIVE terms: ∫∑gₙ = ∑∫gₙ (apply MCT to the partial sums).
    (a) On (0, 1), −ln x ≥ 0 and 1/(1 − x) = ∑_{n≥1} xⁿ⁻¹, so the integrand
    is ∑ gₙ with gₙ = xⁿ⁻¹(−ln x) ≥ 0. By parts (or the substitution
    x = e⁻ᵗ),
       ∫₀¹ xⁿ⁻¹(−ln x) dx = ∫₀^∞ t e^(−nt) dt = 1/n².
    Therefore ∫₀¹ (−ln x)/(1 − x) dx = ∑1/n² = π²/6. (b) For x > 0,
    x/(eˣ − 1) = x e⁻ˣ/(1 − e⁻ˣ) = ∑_{n≥1} x e^(−nx), terms ≥ 0, and ∫₀^∞
    x e^(−nx) dx = 1/n². So the integral is ∑1/n² = π²/6. Without MCT we
    would need uniform convergence, which FAILS near x = 0 in (a). Common
    mistakes: exchanging sum and integral for series with terms of both
    signs without checking ∑∫|gₙ| < ∞. Now you try: ∫₀^∞ x²/(eˣ − 1) dx.
    (2ζ(3) ≈ 2.40411)

D7. State Fatou's lemma and prove it from the MCT. Show that the
    inequality can be strict and that the hypothesis fₙ ≥ 0 cannot be
    dropped.
    Answer: ∫lim inf fₙ ≤ lim inf ∫fₙ; strict for fₙ = n𝟙_(0,1/n); fails for
    fₙ = −n𝟙_(0,1/n).
    Teacher explains: Put gₖ = inf_{n≥k} fₙ. Then 0 ≤ g₁ ≤ g₂ ≤ ... and gₖ ↑
    lim inf fₙ, so by MCT ∫gₖ → ∫lim inf fₙ. But gₖ ≤ fₙ for every n ≥ k,
    so ∫gₖ ≤ inf_{n≥k} ∫fₙ. Letting k → ∞,
       ∫ lim inf fₙ = lim ∫gₖ ≤ lim inf ∫fₙ.  □
    Strict: fₙ = n𝟙_(0,1/n) on [0, 1] has fₙ(x) → 0 for every x, so ∫lim
    inf fₙ = 0, while ∫fₙ = 1 for all n. The mass "escapes" into a
    spike. Negative functions: fₙ = −n𝟙_(0,1/n) has lim inf fₙ = 0 but
    ∫fₙ = −1, and 0 ≤ −1 is false. Picture Fatou as "mass can only be lost
    in the limit, never created". Common mistakes: using lim instead of
    lim inf when the limit does not exist; forgetting that ∫gₖ ≤ ∫fₙ needs
    n ≥ k. Now you try: fₙ = 𝟙_[n,n+1] on R. Compute both sides. (0 < 1)

D8. State the dominated convergence theorem. Evaluate, with full
    justification,
    (a) lim_{n→∞} ∫₀^∞ (1 + x/n)⁻ⁿ sin(x/n) dx,
    (b) lim_{n→∞} ∫₀^n (1 − x/n)ⁿ dx,
    (c) lim_{n→∞} ∫₀¹ n x/(1 + n²x²) dx.
    Answer: (a) 0; (b) 1; (c) 0 (but the integrals behave like
    (ln(1 + n²))/(2n)).
    Teacher explains: DCT: fₙ → f a.e., |fₙ| ≤ g with ∫g < ∞ ⇒ ∫fₙ → ∫f.
    The whole art is finding g. (a) For n ≥ 2, (1 + x/n)ⁿ ≥ 1 + x +
    (n(n − 1)/2)(x/n)² ≥ 1 + x²/4 (binomial theorem, (n − 1)/n ≥ 1/2), so
    |fₙ| ≤ 1/(1 + x²/4) = g, with ∫₀^∞ g = π. Pointwise fₙ(x) → e⁻ˣ · 0 = 0.
    So the limit is 0. (b) Write fₙ = (1 − x/n)ⁿ𝟙_[0,n](x). Since
    1 − t ≤ e⁻ᵗ, 0 ≤ fₙ ≤ e⁻ˣ = g ∈ L¹, and fₙ(x) → e⁻ˣ. Limit ∫₀^∞ e⁻ˣ dx
    = 1. (Check: ∫₀ⁿ(1 − x/n)ⁿ dx = n/(n + 1) → 1 ✓.) (c) fₙ(x) → 0 for
    every x (at x = 0, fₙ = 0). Is there a dominating g? sup_n fₙ(x) = 1/2
    (attained at n = 1/x), so g = 1/2 works on [0, 1]: limit 0. Directly:
    ∫₀¹ nx/(1 + n²x²) dx = ln(1 + n²)/(2n) → 0 ✓. Common mistakes: using the
    pointwise limit without a dominating function; choosing a g that
    depends on n. Now you try: lim ∫₀^∞ n sin(x/n)/(x(1 + x²)) dx. (π/2:
    |fₙ| ≤ 1/(1 + x²), fₙ → 1/(1 + x²))

D9. Using DCT, justify differentiation under the integral sign and compute
    F(t) = ∫₀^∞ e^(−x²) cos(tx) dx by solving an ODE for F.
    Answer: F′(t) = −(t/2)F(t), F(0) = √π/2, so F(t) = (√π/2)e^(−t²/4).
    Teacher explains: THEOREM: if f(·, t) ∈ L¹ for each t, ∂f/∂t exists
    and |∂f/∂t(x, t)| ≤ g(x) ∈ L¹ for all t, then F′(t) = ∫∂f/∂t dx. Proof:
    the difference quotient (f(x, t + h) − f(x, t))/h equals ∂f/∂t(x, ξ)
    by the MVT, so it is bounded by g; let h → 0 along any sequence and
    use DCT. Here ∂f/∂t = −x e^(−x²) sin(tx), bounded by x e^(−x²) ∈ L¹.
    So
       F′(t) = −∫₀^∞ x e^(−x²) sin(tx) dx.
    Integrate by parts with u = sin(tx), dv = −x e^(−x²) dx, v = e^(−x²)/2:
       F′(t) = [e^(−x²) sin(tx)/2]₀^∞ − (t/2)∫₀^∞ e^(−x²) cos(tx) dx
             = −(t/2)F(t).

    dx = √π/2. Check at t = 2: F(2) = (√π/2)e⁻¹ ≈ 0.32603. Common
    mistakes: a bound g that depends on t; forgetting to check that the
    boundary term vanishes. Now you try: G(t) = ∫₀^∞ e^(−tx) dx = 1/t;

D10. State Tonelli's and Fubini's theorems. (a) Compute ∫₀^∞ ∫₀^∞
     e^(−(x²+y²)) dx dy in polar coordinates and deduce ∫₀^∞ e^(−x²) dx.
     (b) Show that for f(x, y) = (x² − y²)/(x² + y²)² on (0, 1)² the two
     iterated integrals are π/4 and −π/4, and explain which hypothesis
     fails.
     Answer: (a) π/4, so ∫₀^∞ e^(−x²) dx = √π/2. (b) ∬|f| = ∞.
     Teacher explains: TONELLI (f ≥ 0 measurable, σ-finite spaces): both
     iterated integrals equal the double integral, finite or not. FUBINI:
     the same if ∬|f| < ∞. (a) The integrand is positive, so Tonelli lets
     us compute in any order or coordinates:
        ∬_{first quadrant} e^(−r²) r dr dθ = (π/2)·(1/2) = π/4.
     The left side is (∫₀^∞ e^(−x²) dx)², so the integral is √π/2.
     (b) ∂/∂y (y/(x² + y²)) = (x² − y²)/(x² + y²)². Hence
        ∫₀¹ f(x, y) dy = [y/(x² + y²)]₀¹ = 1/(1 + x²),
     and ∫₀¹ dx/(1 + x²) = π/4. By the antisymmetry f(y, x) = −f(x, y), the
     other order gives −π/4. Fubini does not apply: in polar coordinates
     |f| = |cos 2θ|/r², and ∬|f| r dr dθ contains ∫₀ dr/r = ∞ near the
     origin. Common mistakes: swapping the order without checking
     integrability; thinking Fubini fails for positive functions (Tonelli
     always works for f ≥ 0). Now you try: compute ∫₀¹∫₀¹ xy dx dy both
     ways. (1/4)

D11. Explain the four modes of convergence (uniform, a.e., in measure, in
     L¹) with the "typewriter" sequence, the escaping bump 𝟙_[n,n+1] and the
     spike n𝟙_(0,1/n). State Egoroff's theorem and apply it to xⁿ on [0, 1].
     Answer: typewriter: L¹ and in measure, nowhere a.e.; escaping bump:
     pointwise to 0, not in measure (infinite space) nor L¹; spike: a.e. and
     in measure, not L¹.
     Teacher explains: TYPEWRITER: list the intervals [0, 1], [0, ½], [½, 1],
     [0, ⅓], [⅓, ⅔], [⅔, 1], ... and let fₙ be the indicator of the n-th.
     ∫fₙ = length → 0, so fₙ → 0 in L¹ and in measure. But every x lies in
     infinitely many and misses infinitely many intervals, so fₙ(x)
     oscillates between 0 and 1 for every x: no pointwise limit. (A
     subsequence — the intervals [0, 1/k] — does converge a.e., as the
     theory predicts.) ESCAPING BUMP on R: fₙ(x) → 0 for every x, but
     m{fₙ = 1} = 1 for all n: not in measure, ∫fₙ = 1 ↛ 0. Egoroff needs
     finite measure. SPIKE n𝟙_(0,1/n) on [0, 1]: → 0 everywhere, m{fₙ > ε}
     = 1/n → 0 (in measure), but ∫fₙ = 1: not L¹. EGOROFF: on a finite
     measure space, fₙ → f a.e. ⇒ for every δ > 0 there is E with m(E) < δ
     and fₙ → f uniformly off E. For xⁿ on [0, 1]: take E = (1 − δ, 1];
     on [0, 1 − δ], sup xⁿ = (1 − δ)ⁿ → 0. Common mistakes: believing a.e.
     convergence implies L¹ convergence. Now you try: does fₙ = 𝟙_[0,1/n]
     converge a.e.? in L¹? (yes to 0 except at x = 0; yes)

D12. Show that if f ∈ L¹(R) then (a) for every ε > 0 there is δ > 0 such
     that m(A) < δ ⇒ ∫_A|f| < ε; (b) F(x) = ∫_{−∞}^x f is uniformly
     continuous; (c) lim_{h→0} ∫|f(x + h) − f(x)| dx = 0 (continuity of
     translation), first for f = 𝟙_[a,b], then in general.
     Answer: (a) truncate f at level N; (b) |F(x) − F(y)| ≤ ∫_[x,y]|f|;
     (c) ∫|𝟙_[a,b](x + h) − 𝟙_[a,b](x)| dx = 2|h| for |h| ≤ b − a, then
     approximate.
     Teacher explains: (a) Let fₙ = min(|f|, N). By MCT, ∫fₙ ↑ ∫|f|, so
     choose N with ∫(|f| − min(|f|, N)) < ε/2. Then for m(A) < δ = ε/(2N),
        ∫_A|f| ≤ ∫(|f| − min(|f|, N)) + ∫_A N < ε/2 + Nδ = ε.
     (b) For x < y, |F(y) − F(x)| ≤ ∫_{[x,y]}|f| < ε whenever y − x < δ,
     by (a). (c) For f = 𝟙_[a,b] and 0 < h ≤ b − a, f(x + h) − f(x) is ±1
     exactly on [a − h, a) and [b − h, b), so the integral is 2h → 0. By
     linearity the same holds for step functions. Step functions are
     dense in L¹: given ε, pick a step s with ∫|f − s| < ε; then
        ∫|f(x + h) − f(x)| ≤ ∫|f(x + h) − s(x + h)| + ∫|s(x + h) − s(x)|
                             + ∫|s − f| < 3ε for small h.
     This "ε/3 argument" is the model for many proofs in 11.2 and 11.3.
     Common mistakes: using uniform continuity of f (f need not be
     continuous). Now you try: compute ∫|𝟙_[0,1](x + h) − 𝟙_[0,1](x)| dx for
     h = 3. (2: the intervals no longer overlap)

M12. True (T) or false (F)? Give a counterexample for F.
     a) Every subset of a Lebesgue null set is Lebesgue measurable.
     b) Every Lebesgue measurable set is a Borel set.
     c) A set of Lebesgue measure 0 is countable.
     d) An open dense subset of [0, 1] has measure 1.
     e) If ∫|f| = 0 then f = 0 everywhere.
     f) If fₙ → f pointwise on [0, 1] then ∫fₙ → ∫f.
     g) If fₙ ↓ f ≥ 0 pointwise then ∫fₙ → ∫f.
     h) Convergence in L¹ implies convergence a.e.
     i) On [0, 1], a.e. convergence implies convergence in measure.
     j) The two iterated integrals agree for every measurable f on [0, 1]².
     k) Tonelli's theorem holds for every measurable f ≥ 0 on a product of
        σ-finite spaces.
     l) A bounded f on [a, b] is Riemann integrable iff it is continuous
        a.e.
     m) If f ∈ L¹(R) then f(x) → 0 as x → ∞.
     n) If f ∈ L¹(R) is uniformly continuous then f(x) → 0 as x → ±∞.
     o) A continuous function maps null sets to null sets.
     p) A Lipschitz function R → R maps null sets to null sets.
     q) If m(E) > 0 then E − E = {x − y : x, y ∈ E} contains an interval
        around 0.
     r) Every Lebesgue measurable function is a.e. equal to a Borel function.
     Answers:
       a) T (completeness)
       b) F: a non-Borel subset of the Cantor set (there are 2^𝔠 subsets of

       e) F: 𝟙_Q                     f) F: n𝟙_(0,1/n)
       g) F: 𝟙_[n,∞) (true if ∫f₁ < ∞)
       h) F: typewriter (a subsequence does converge a.e.)
       i) T                          j) F: M8 h)
       k) T                          l) T (Lebesgue's criterion)
       m) F: a spike of height 1 and width 2⁻ⁿ at each integer n
       n) T                          o) F: the Cantor function maps C onto
                                        [0, 1]
       p) T                          q) T (Steinhaus theorem)
       r) T

TEACHING TIPS
  Struggling: start every convergence question by DRAWING fₙ for n = 1,
  2, 5, 20 and asking "where does the area go?" — a spike (n𝟙_(0,1/n)),
  a wanderer (𝟙_[n,n+1]) or a flattening (1/n)𝟙_[0,n]); these three
  pictures explain every failure of "lim ∫ = ∫ lim". Insist on a written
  line "0 ≤ fₙ ↑ f, so MCT" or "|fₙ| ≤ g with ∫g = ..., so DCT" before any
  exchange of limit and integral, and "∬|f| < ∞ by Tonelli, so Fubini"
  before any change of order. For σ-algebras on finite sets, always find
  the atoms first. Harder: prove Carathéodory's theorem that the
  measurable sets form a σ-algebra and m is countably additive on it;
  prove that Lebesgue measure is the only translation-invariant Borel
  measure with m[0, 1] = 1; construct the Cantor function and show it is
  continuous, increasing, with derivative 0 a.e. yet c(0) = 0, c(1) = 1
  (so the fundamental theorem of calculus fails without absolute
  continuity); prove Egoroff and Lusin's theorems; prove the Lebesgue
  differentiation theorem via the Vitali covering lemma; and show that
  convergence in measure is metrisable but a.e. convergence is not.
