================================================================================
        STAGE 0: PRE-NURSERY (Age ~2-3) — QUESTIONS AND ANSWERS
        Sensing quantity, shape and pattern
================================================================================

How to use this file (note for the parent / teacher)
----------------------------------------------------
- Each of the 17 topics from topic.txt (0.1 to 0.17) has 2 questions:
    Q1 = DESCRIPTIVE : builds the idea and the words for it.
    Q2 = NUMERICAL   : uses small numbers (1 to 5, sometimes up to 10).
- Every question has:
    * SET-UP          what to keep ready (use real objects, never worksheets
                      at this age)
    * ASK             the exact words to say to the child
    * EXPECTED ANSWER what a child who understands will say or do
    * TEACHER'S EXPLANATION  how to explain it, step by step
    * IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES  an easier version to try
    * TRY NEXT        a slightly harder version for the next day
- Children aged 2-3 mostly answer by pointing, picking up, or saying one or
  two words. A pointing answer counts as a correct answer.
- Sessions should be 5-10 minutes, playful, with praise for effort
  ("You looked so carefully!"), never pressure.
- Repeat each question on different days with different objects. Real
  understanding shows when the child can do it with NEW objects.

TOPIC 0.1 — SAME AND DIFFERENT
================================================================================

Q1 (DESCRIPTIVE)
SET-UP : Two red cups that look exactly alike and one blue spoon.
ASK    : "Look at these. Which two are the SAME? Which one is DIFFERENT?
          Why is it different?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  The child points to or picks up the two red cups as "same" and points to
  the spoon as "different". Reasons the child may give: "That's a spoon",
  "It's blue", "Not a cup".
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Hold up the two cups side by side: "This is a cup. This is a cup. Both
     are red. They look the same. They are the SAME."
  2. Hold up the spoon: "This is a spoon. It is not a cup. It is blue. It is
     DIFFERENT."
  3. Say the rule in one sentence: "SAME means they match. DIFFERENT means
     they do not match."
  4. Ask the child to say the words back: "Same!" "Different!"
  Why it matters: noticing same and different is the first step of all
  mathematics. Sorting, counting, patterns and later algebra all begin with
  deciding whether two things are "the same kind".
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Use two things that are very different, e.g. a shoe and a banana, next to
  two identical bananas. Only use one difference at a time (shape OR colour).
TRY NEXT:
  Two red cups and one red bowl (same colour, different object) — the child
  must notice the shape difference, not the colour.

Q2 (NUMERICAL)
SET-UP : 4 socks: 2 with stars and 2 with stripes, all mixed up.
ASK    : "Can you find the pairs? How many socks go together in one pair?
          How many pairs did we make?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  The child puts the 2 star socks together and the 2 striped socks
  together.
  - Socks in one pair = 2
  - Number of pairs = 2
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Pick one star sock. "Where is the sock that is the SAME as this one?"
  2. Put them together. Count by touching: "one, two. Two socks make ONE
     pair."
  3. Do the same with the striped socks. "One, two. Another pair."
  4. Point to each pair: "One pair, two pairs. We made 2 pairs."
  So: 4 socks = 2 pairs, and each pair has 2 socks.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Start with only 1 pair and 1 odd sock. Just ask, "Which two match?"
TRY NEXT:
  6 socks (3 pairs), or shoes: "Mummy's shoes, Papa's shoes, your shoes."

TOPIC 0.2 — SORTING BY ONE ATTRIBUTE (colour, size, shape, type)
================================================================================

Q1 (DESCRIPTIVE)
SET-UP : A mix of 3 red blocks and 3 yellow blocks. Two bowls: one red, one
         yellow.
ASK    : "Let's help the blocks go home. Red blocks live in the red bowl.
          Yellow blocks live in the yellow bowl. Where does this one go?
          How did you know?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  The child puts every red block in the red bowl and every yellow block in
  the yellow bowl. When asked "how did you know?", says "Red!" or points to
  the colour.
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. "When we SORT, we put things that are the SAME together."
  2. "Today we are sorting by COLOUR. We only look at the colour."
  3. Pick up a block, say its colour aloud, then place it: "Red. Red goes
     here." Let the child copy you.
  4. At the end, check together: "Are all the red ones together? Yes!"
  Why it matters: sorting teaches the child to pay attention to one
  property at a time. Later this becomes sets, classification and data.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Sort only 2 blocks, one of each colour, and make the bowls match the
  colours so the answer is easy to see.
TRY NEXT:
  Sort the SAME blocks a new way — by size (big/small) or shape
  (cube/ball). This teaches that one group can be sorted by different rules.

Q2 (NUMERICAL)
SET-UP : 2 toy cars and 3 toy animals mixed on a mat.
ASK    : "Put the cars in this box and the animals in that box. How many
          cars? How many animals? Which box has more?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  - Cars = 2
  - Animals = 3
  - The animal box has more.
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Sort by TYPE: "Is it a car or an animal?" Put each in its box.
  2. Count the cars by touching each one: "one, two" — 2 cars.
  3. Count the animals the same way: "one, two, three" — 3 animals.
  4. Line them up one car next to one animal. One animal has no partner,
     so the animals are MORE. 3 is more than 2.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Use only 1 car and 2 animals; stop at sorting and just say the counts
  aloud yourself.
TRY NEXT:
  Sort 5 fruits into apples and bananas and count each group.

TOPIC 0.3 — BIG / SMALL, TALL / SHORT, LONG / SHORT, HEAVY / LIGHT
================================================================================

Q1 (DESCRIPTIVE)
SET-UP : A big ball and a small ball; a long ribbon and a short ribbon;
         a heavy book and a light feather (or cotton ball).
ASK    : "Show me the BIG ball. Which ribbon is LONG? Hold these two. Which
          one is HEAVY? Which is LIGHT?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  Points to the big ball, the long ribbon, and says the book is heavy and
  the feather is light (may show this by making a "heavy" face or dropping
  the arm).
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. BIG/SMALL is about how much space something takes. "The big ball fills
     both my hands. The small ball fits in one hand."
  2. LONG/SHORT is about how far something stretches. Lay the ribbons side
     by side with ends lined up at one side: "This one goes further. It is
     LONG. This one stops early. It is SHORT."
  3. TALL/SHORT is the same idea but standing up: "You are tall next to
     your teddy. Papa is tall next to you."
  4. HEAVY/LIGHT is about how hard something pulls down in your hand.
     "The book pushes my hand down — HEAVY. The feather hardly pushes —
     LIGHT."
  Important: always line up the ends when comparing length. This is the
  first idea of measuring.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Compare only one pair per day and choose objects with a huge difference
  (a big watermelon and a tiny grape).
TRY NEXT:
  Ask the opposite words: "Show me the SMALL one", "Which ribbon is SHORT?"

Q2 (NUMERICAL)
SET-UP : 3 teddies: small, medium, big.
ASK    : "How many teddies are there? Which one is the biggest? Which one is
          the smallest? Can you put them in a line from small to big?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  - There are 3 teddies.
  - Points correctly to the biggest and the smallest.
  - Lines them up: small, medium, big.
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Count by touching: "one, two, three teddies."
  2. "Which is the biggest? Let's stand them next to each other." The one
     whose head is highest is biggest.
  3. The smallest has the lowest head.
  4. The one left over goes in the MIDDLE: it is bigger than the small one
     but smaller than the big one.
  5. Say the line together: "Small, bigger, biggest!"
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Use only 2 teddies (big and small) before adding the middle one.
TRY NEXT:
  4 cups of different sizes in order; or 3 pencils from short to long.

TOPIC 0.4 — MORE / LESS / SAME (by looking, without counting)
================================================================================

Q1 (DESCRIPTIVE)
SET-UP : Two plates. Plate A has a big pile of grapes (about 10). Plate B
         has a few (about 2).
ASK    : "Which plate has MORE grapes? Which plate has LESS? How can you
          tell?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  Points to Plate A for more and Plate B for less. Explains: "Big pile",
  "Lots", "That one has only a little".
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. "When one group has MORE, there are extra things in it."
  2. "When one group has LESS (or FEWER), some are missing."
  3. "If both have the SAME, nothing is extra and nothing is missing."
  4. At this age we judge by LOOKING. The difference should be big and
     easy to see; exact counting comes later.
  5. Ask a fun follow-up: "Which plate would you like?" — children almost
     always choose MORE, which shows they understand.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Make the difference even bigger (10 vs 1), or use very large objects
  like oranges.
TRY NEXT:
  Show two plates with the SAME number (3 and 3, arranged the same way)
  and teach the word SAME.

Q2 (NUMERICAL)
SET-UP : Row A: 3 biscuits. Row B: 1 biscuit. Place each biscuit in Row B
         directly under one in Row A.
ASK    : "Which row has more biscuits? How many biscuits must I add to Row B
          so both rows are the SAME?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  - Row A has more.
  - We must add 2 biscuits to Row B. (3 and 3 are the same.)
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Point under each biscuit in Row A. "This biscuit has a friend below.
     These two have NO friends below." So Row A has more.
  2. "Let's give them friends." Add 1 biscuit to Row B: "one". Add another:
     "two".
  3. Now every biscuit in Row A has a friend. Both rows have 3. They are
     the SAME.
  4. So we needed to add 2.
  (This is the child's first taste of "3 is 2 more than 1".)
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Use 2 vs 1 — only one "friend" is missing.
TRY NEXT:
  4 vs 2. Or start with the same number and ask, "I took one away. Which
  row has less now?"

TOPIC 0.5 — FULL / EMPTY, A LOT / A LITTLE
================================================================================

Q1 (DESCRIPTIVE)
SET-UP : Two clear glasses. One is full of water, one is empty. A small
         bowl of rice.
ASK    : "Which glass is FULL? Which is EMPTY? Can you put A LITTLE rice
          in the empty bowl? Now put A LOT of rice."
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  Points correctly to full and empty. Puts a pinch of rice for "a little"
  and several handfuls for "a lot".
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. "FULL means the glass cannot hold any more. The water comes to the
     top."
  2. "EMPTY means there is nothing inside." Turn it upside-down: nothing
     comes out.
  3. "A LITTLE is a small amount. A LOT is a big amount."
  4. Between empty and full there is "half full" — mention it, but don't
     expect the child to use it yet.
  Why it matters: this is the start of measuring CAPACITY (how much a
  container holds) and of the idea of zero (empty = nothing).
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Let the child pour water themselves in a bath or bucket and say "full!"
  when it reaches the top.
TRY NEXT:
  Show three glasses: empty, half full, full. "Put them in order from
  empty to full."

Q2 (NUMERICAL)
SET-UP : An empty basket and 5 balls.
ASK    : "The basket is empty. How many balls are in it? Now put in balls
          one by one and count. How many are in it now? Take them all out.
          How many now?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  - Empty basket: 0 (no balls; "none", "nothing").
  - After putting all in: 5 balls.
  - After taking all out: 0 again.
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Look inside: "No balls. Empty. That is ZERO balls."
  2. Put each ball in and count aloud together: "one, two, three, four,
     five." "Now it has 5 balls — it is full of balls."
  3. Take each out: "five, four, three, two, one ... none left. Zero."
  4. Key idea: EMPTY means ZERO.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Use 2 or 3 balls only. Stress the word "none" before the word "zero".
TRY NEXT:
  "Put 2 balls in. Is the basket empty? No! Is it full? Not yet — we can
  fit more."

TOPIC 0.6 — POSITION WORDS (in, on, under, over, beside, front, behind,
            top, bottom)
================================================================================

Q1 (DESCRIPTIVE)
SET-UP : A box, a teddy bear, and a table.
ASK    : "Put teddy IN the box. Now put teddy ON the box. Put teddy UNDER
          the table. Put teddy BESIDE the box. Where is teddy now?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  Places the teddy correctly each time. When asked "Where is teddy?",
  answers with a position word: "Under!", "In box!"
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Use your own body first: "I am standing IN the room. My hand is ON my
     head. My feet are UNDER the table."
  2. Then use the teddy. Do one word at a time and say the full sentence:
     "Teddy is IN the box."
  3. Pairs of opposites help: in/out, on/under, over/under, front/behind,
     top/bottom.
  4. Play "Simon Says" with position: "Put your hands ON your knees!"
  Why it matters: these words are the start of GEOMETRY and describing
  where things are (later: coordinates, maps, directions).
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Teach only IN and ON first. Add UNDER after a few days.
TRY NEXT:
  FRONT and BEHIND: "Stand in FRONT of the chair. Now hide BEHIND it."

Q2 (NUMERICAL)
SET-UP : A shelf or a stack with 3 levels: TOP, MIDDLE, BOTTOM. Put 1 car on
         the top, 2 cars in the middle, and 3 cars at the bottom.
ASK    : "How many cars are on the TOP shelf? How many on the BOTTOM shelf?
          Which shelf has the most cars?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  - Top shelf: 1 car.
  - Bottom shelf: 3 cars.
  - The bottom shelf has the most.
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Point: "This is the TOP — the highest. This is the BOTTOM — the
     lowest."
  2. Count the top: "one" — 1 car.
  3. Count the bottom by touching: "one, two, three" — 3 cars.
  4. (Middle has 2.) "3 is the most, so the BOTTOM shelf has the most."
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Use just TOP and BOTTOM with 1 and 2 cars.
TRY NEXT:
  "Put 2 blocks UNDER the chair and 1 block ON the chair. How many blocks
  altogether?" (Answer: 3.)

TOPIC 0.7 — ROTE COUNTING SONGS AND RHYMES (1 to 5, then 1 to 10)
================================================================================

Q1 (DESCRIPTIVE)
SET-UP : No materials. Your hands.
ASK    : "Let's sing our counting song! 'One, two, buckle my shoe; three,
          four, knock at the door; five, six, pick up sticks...' What number
          comes after THREE?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  Sings along. Says "Four!" for the number after three.
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Counting words always come in the SAME ORDER: one, two, three, four,
     five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten. This order never changes.
  2. Songs and rhymes help the child remember the order, just like the
     alphabet song.
  3. Show the fingers while singing so each word goes with a finger.
  4. Be honest with yourself: saying the numbers in order (rote counting)
     is NOT yet the same as knowing "how many". That comes in Topic 0.10
     and Stage 1. Rote counting is the foundation.
  Good rhymes: "One, Two, Buckle My Shoe", "Five Little Monkeys",
  "Five Little Ducks", "Ten in the Bed", and counting rhymes in the
  child's home language.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Stop at 3, then 5. Sing slowly and let the child fill in the last word:
  "One, two, ..." ("three!").
TRY NEXT:
  Count to 10. Then play "What comes next?" starting from any number:
  "Five, six, ..." ("seven!").

Q2 (NUMERICAL)
SET-UP : "Five Little Monkeys Jumping on the Bed" using 5 fingers.
ASK    : "Five little monkeys jumping on the bed, one fell off and bumped
          his head... How many monkeys are left now?" (Continue until none
          are left.)
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  5 -> 4 -> 3 -> 2 -> 1 -> 0 ("no little monkeys").
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Hold up 5 fingers. "Five monkeys."
  2. Fold down one finger each time one falls: "Now how many?" Count the
     fingers left if needed.
  3. Point out the pattern: each time ONE falls off, the number goes one
     step BACK: 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0.
  4. This is the child's first counting BACKWARDS and first "take away".
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Start with "Three little monkeys" and count the fingers together every
  time.
TRY NEXT:
  "Ten in the Bed" — counting back from 10.

TOPIC 0.8 — SUBITISING SMALL GROUPS (instantly seeing 1, 2, 3 items)
================================================================================

Q1 (DESCRIPTIVE)
SET-UP : Cards with dots: 1 dot, 2 dots, 3 dots (large, clear dots). Or a
         die showing 1, 2 and 3.
ASK    : (Show a card for just 2 seconds, then hide it.) "Quick! How many
          dots did you see? How did you know so fast?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  Says the correct number without counting one by one. Explains: "I saw
  it!", "Two, like my eyes."
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Our eyes can see very small groups — 1, 2 or 3 — all at once, without
     counting. This is called SUBITISING ("sudden seeing").
  2. Show common shapes of numbers:
       1 = a single dot            •
       2 = a pair, like two eyes   •  •
       3 = a triangle              •
                                  •  •
  3. Connect to the body: 1 nose, 2 eyes, 2 hands, 3 wheels on a tricycle.
  4. Seeing small numbers quickly makes later adding much faster.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Show the card longer and count the dots together, then show it quickly
  again. Use only 1 and 2 at first.
TRY NEXT:
  Show 3 dots in different arrangements (in a line, in a triangle) so the
  child learns that 3 is 3 in any shape. Later, try 4 (a square).

Q2 (NUMERICAL)
SET-UP : A handful of buttons. Quickly drop 2 buttons on the table, then
         cover them with your hand after 2 seconds.
ASK    : "How many buttons were there? Now I'll add 1 more (drop 1 more and
          show quickly). How many now?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  - First: 2 buttons.
  - After adding one more: 3 buttons.
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. "You saw 2 — a pair."
  2. "I put one more. 2 and 1 more makes 3."
  3. Lift your hand and check by touching: "one, two, three. Yes, 3!"
  4. Checking by counting teaches the child that seeing and counting give
     the SAME answer.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Keep the buttons visible (don't cover) and just ask "how many?"
TRY NEXT:
  Drop 1, then 2 more (1 and 2 make 3). Or roll a die with 1, 2, 3 and
  name the number without counting.

TOPIC 0.9 — CONSERVATION OF NUMBER (spreading items out does not change
            how many)
================================================================================

Note for the teacher: many children aged 2-4 will say a spread-out line has
"more". This is completely normal for their age. Don't correct harshly;
count together and let them discover it over many weeks.

Q1 (DESCRIPTIVE)
SET-UP : 4 toy cars in a tight group. Then spread the same 4 cars out in a
         long line.
ASK    : "I didn't add any cars. I didn't take any away. I only moved them.
          Are there more cars now, less cars, or the same?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  "Same!" (A child who says "more" is not yet ready — count together.)
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. "Watch my hands. I am not adding. I am not taking away. I am only
     moving."
  2. Count the cars in the group: "1, 2, 3, 4."
  3. Spread them out and count again: "1, 2, 3, 4."
  4. "Still 4! Moving things does not change how many there are. Only
     adding or taking away changes how many."
  5. Push them back together and count again: "Still 4!"
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Use only 2 cars. Let the child move them apart and together and count
  each time.
TRY NEXT:
  Two rows of 3 sweets, one row spread out longer. "Which row has more?"
  (Same — both 3.)

Q2 (NUMERICAL)
SET-UP : 3 blocks in a tower. Then take the tower apart and lay the blocks
         in a row.
ASK    : "How many blocks in the tower? Now how many blocks in the row?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  - Tower: 3 blocks.
  - Row: 3 blocks. It is the same number.
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Count the tower from bottom to top: "1, 2, 3."
  2. Take it apart one block at a time and put them in a row.
  3. Count the row: "1, 2, 3."
  4. "Tall tower or long row, it is still 3 blocks."
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  2 blocks only; count with the child's finger on each block.
TRY NEXT:
  5 blocks: tower, then row, then a circle. Ask before counting: "Do you
  think it is still 5?"

TOPIC 0.10 — ONE-TO-ONE CORRESPONDENCE (one plate per person, one sock per
             foot)
================================================================================

Q1 (DESCRIPTIVE)
SET-UP : 3 dolls (or family members) and some spoons.
ASK    : "Each doll needs ONE spoon. Can you give every doll a spoon? Does
          anyone have no spoon? Does anyone have two?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  Gives exactly one spoon to each doll. Says "Everybody has one!"
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. "ONE for each: one doll, one spoon."
  2. Go doll by doll: "Spoon for you. Spoon for you. Spoon for you."
  3. Check: "Does every doll have one? Does anyone have two? No! Good."
  4. This is the secret behind counting correctly: when we count, we say
     ONE number for EACH object, touching each thing exactly once. If we
     skip one or touch one twice, the count goes wrong.
  Everyday practice: setting the table, one sock per foot, one shoe per
  foot, one sticker per page.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  2 dolls, 2 spoons. Guide the child's hand.
TRY NEXT:
  Give the child more spoons than dolls: "Did we have extra spoons?"

Q2 (NUMERICAL)
SET-UP : 4 cups and 3 saucers.
ASK    : "Put one cup on each saucer. How many cups have a saucer? How many
          cups are left without a saucer?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  - 3 cups have a saucer.
  - 1 cup is left without a saucer.
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Place one cup on each saucer: "one, two, three."
  2. "3 cups are sitting on saucers."
  3. One cup is left: "This cup has no saucer. 1 cup is left over."
  4. "So there are MORE cups than saucers. 4 cups, 3 saucers."
  This matching idea is how we compare numbers before we know the
  written numbers.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  3 cups and 2 saucers.
TRY NEXT:
  Match children to chairs in a game ("musical chairs" style): how many
  are left standing?

TOPIC 0.11 — BASIC SHAPES BY NAME: CIRCLE, SQUARE, TRIANGLE
================================================================================

Q1 (DESCRIPTIVE)
SET-UP : Cardboard cut-outs of a circle, a square and a triangle (all the
         same colour so the child looks at the shape, not the colour).
ASK    : "Show me the CIRCLE. Show me the TRIANGLE. What does a circle feel
          like when you run your finger around it? What does a triangle
          have?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  Picks up the right shapes. Says the circle is "round" / "no corners"
  and the triangle has "points" / "corners".
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. CIRCLE: round all the way, like a ball seen flat, a plate, a wheel.
     Run the child's finger around it: no bumps, no corners.
  2. SQUARE: 4 straight sides, all the same length, 4 corners. Like a
     window, a biscuit, a slice of bread.
  3. TRIANGLE: 3 straight sides, 3 corners (points). Like a slice of
     pizza or a samosa.
  4. Go on a "shape hunt" around the house: clock (circle), tile (square),
     roof (triangle).
  Important: turn the triangle upside-down or sideways — it is still a
  triangle! Shapes keep their name when you turn them.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Learn circle only for a few days, then add square, then triangle.
TRY NEXT:
  Show a big triangle and a small triangle: "Are both triangles?" (Yes.
  Size doesn't change the shape.)

Q2 (NUMERICAL)
SET-UP : The triangle and the square cut-outs.
ASK    : "Let's count the corners. How many corners does the triangle have?
          How many corners does the square have? How many corners does the
          circle have?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  - Triangle: 3 corners.
  - Square: 4 corners.
  - Circle: 0 corners (none).
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Put the child's finger on one corner of the triangle and count each
     corner once: "1, 2, 3." (Mark the first corner with a sticker so you
     don't count it twice.)
  2. Do the same for the square: "1, 2, 3, 4."
  3. Circle: go around — "no corners anywhere. Zero corners."
  4. "TRI-angle has 3 corners. The square has 4. The circle has none."
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Count only the triangle's corners. Clap once per corner.
TRY NEXT:
  Count the SIDES too: a triangle has 3 sides, a square has 4 sides.

TOPIC 0.12 — SIMPLE REPEATING PATTERNS (red-blue-red-blue; clap-stomp-clap)
================================================================================

Q1 (DESCRIPTIVE)
SET-UP : Beads or blocks laid out: RED, BLUE, RED, BLUE, RED, ___
ASK    : "Look at my line. Red, blue, red, blue, red... What comes next?
          Why?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  "Blue!" Because "it goes red, blue, red, blue" / "it takes turns".
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. "A PATTERN is something that REPEATS again and again in the same
     order."
  2. Read the pattern aloud with rhythm: "RED-blue, RED-blue, RED-..."
     The rhythm helps the child hear what comes next.
  3. Find the part that repeats (the "core"): here it is "red, blue".
  4. Patterns are everywhere: day-night-day-night, stripes on a shirt,
     clap-stomp-clap-stomp.
  Why it matters: patterns are the root of algebra. Mathematicians spend
  their lives looking for patterns.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Use actions first: clap, stomp, clap, stomp... Let the child do the
  next action.
TRY NEXT:
  Let the child MAKE their own pattern and explain it to you. Then try an
  AAB pattern: clap, clap, stomp.

Q2 (NUMERICAL)
SET-UP : The pattern: CLAP, STOMP, CLAP, STOMP, CLAP, STOMP.
ASK    : "Let's do it together. How many claps did we do? How many stomps?
          If we do the pattern one more time, how many claps will there be?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  - Claps = 3.
  - Stomps = 3.
  - One more time: 4 claps (and 4 stomps).
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Do it slowly. Hold up a finger for each clap: "1, 2, 3 claps."
  2. Do it again, counting stomps: "1, 2, 3 stomps."
  3. "Each time we do 'clap, stomp' we get one more clap and one more
     stomp. So they are always the SAME number."
  4. One more round: 3 claps + 1 more = 4 claps.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Do the pattern only twice (2 claps, 2 stomps).
TRY NEXT:
  "Clap, clap, stomp" done twice: how many claps? (4) How many stomps? (2)

TOPIC 0.13 — STACKING AND NESTING (blocks, cups) — early sense of size order
================================================================================

Q1 (DESCRIPTIVE)
SET-UP : A set of 4 nesting cups (or stacking rings) of different sizes.
ASK    : "Can you put all the cups inside each other? Which cup do you put
          first? Why does the big one not fit inside the small one?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  Nests them correctly (may use trial and error). Explains: "Too big!",
  "Small goes in big."
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. "A SMALLER thing can fit INSIDE a BIGGER thing. A bigger thing cannot
     fit inside a smaller one."
  2. To nest: start with the BIGGEST cup, then the next biggest inside it,
     and so on — or start with the smallest inside the next one up.
  3. To build a tower (stacking cups upside-down), put the BIGGEST at the
     bottom and the smallest on top; otherwise the tower falls.
  4. This is ORDERING by size — the same skill used later when putting
     numbers in order (1, 2, 3, 4...).
  Let the child make mistakes and fix them; trial and error IS learning.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Give only 2 cups (very different sizes), then 3.
TRY NEXT:
  Build a tower with the biggest at the bottom; then ask "Which one should
  go on top?"

Q2 (NUMERICAL)
SET-UP : 5 stacking blocks (or 5 rings of a stacking toy).
ASK    : "Build a tower. Count the blocks as you put them on. How tall is
          your tower in blocks? If I take off the top block, how many are
          left?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  - Tower = 5 blocks.
  - After taking one off: 4 blocks.
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Count each block as it goes on: "1, 2, 3, 4, 5." The last number said
     tells how many: 5 blocks tall.
  2. Take off the top block: "One went away." Count again: "1, 2, 3, 4."
  3. "5 take away 1 is 4. The tower got shorter by one block."
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Tower of 3 blocks; after taking one away, 2 are left.
TRY NEXT:
  Two towers: one of 3 blocks, one of 5. "Which is taller? How many more
  blocks does the short tower need to be the same?" (2.)

TOPIC 0.14 — DAILY ROUTINE SEQUENCE: BEFORE / AFTER, FIRST / NEXT / LAST
================================================================================

Q1 (DESCRIPTIVE)
SET-UP : Three pictures (drawn or printed): waking up, brushing teeth,
         eating breakfast. Mix them up.
ASK    : "What do we do FIRST in the morning? What do we do NEXT? What do we
          do LAST? Can you put the pictures in order?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  Orders them: 1) wake up, 2) brush teeth, 3) eat breakfast. Uses words
  like "first", "then", "last".
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. "Things happen in an ORDER, one after another."
  2. "FIRST is the start. NEXT (or THEN) comes after. LAST is the end."
  3. "BEFORE means earlier. We brush teeth BEFORE breakfast. AFTER means
     later. We eat breakfast AFTER brushing."
  4. Talk through the day often: "First bath, then pyjamas, then story,
     last sleep."
  Why it matters: sequencing is the basis of ordinal numbers (1st, 2nd,
  3rd), time, and later following the steps of a calculation.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Use only 2 pictures: "wake up" and "sleep". Which comes first?
TRY NEXT:
  4 pictures (wash hands -> eat -> wash plate -> play). Ask "What comes
  BEFORE eating?"

Q2 (NUMERICAL)
SET-UP : 3 children (or 3 toys) standing in a line for a slide.
ASK    : "Who is FIRST in the line? Who is LAST? How many are in the line?
          If the first one goes down the slide, how many are still waiting?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  - Points to the first and the last correctly.
  - 3 in the line.
  - After the first goes: 2 still waiting.
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. "First is at the front, nearest the slide. Last is at the back."
  2. Count the line: "1, 2, 3."
  3. First one goes: "Wheee!" Count again: "1, 2." "2 are still waiting."
  4. "The one who was second is now FIRST!"
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Only 2 toys in the line.
TRY NEXT:
  4 in the line; 2 go down the slide. How many wait? (2.)


================================================================================
TOPIC 0.15 — SIMPLE PUZZLES (2-6 piece), SHAPE SORTERS
================================================================================

Q1 (DESCRIPTIVE)
SET-UP : A shape-sorter box with holes for a circle, square and triangle,
         and the matching blocks.
ASK    : "Which hole does this block go in? Why won't the square go into
          the circle hole?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  Posts each block through the right hole (with some trying). Explains:
  "Wrong shape", "The corners don't fit".
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. "Look at the block. Look at the holes. Find the hole with the SAME
     shape."
  2. Run the child's finger around the edge of the block and then around
     the edge of the hole — they feel the same.
  3. "The square has corners. The circle hole has no corners, so the
     square gets stuck."
  4. Sometimes you must TURN the block to make it fit — this is the first
     idea of rotation.
  Puzzles teach looking at shapes and edges, trying, and not giving up.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Cover all holes except one with tape. Give only the matching block.
TRY NEXT:
  A 4-piece jigsaw of a familiar animal. Ask "Where is the head piece?"

Q2 (NUMERICAL)
SET-UP : A 4-piece jigsaw puzzle.
ASK    : "How many pieces does this puzzle have? You put 1 piece in. How
          many pieces are still left to do?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  - Puzzle has 4 pieces.
  - After placing 1: 3 pieces left.
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Line up the pieces and count by touching: "1, 2, 3, 4 pieces."
  2. After one is placed, count the ones still on the table: "1, 2, 3."
  3. "4 pieces, 1 done, 3 more to go."
  4. Keep counting down as each piece goes in: 3, 2, 1, 0 — "Finished!"
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Use a 2-piece puzzle: 1 placed, 1 left.
TRY NEXT:
  6-piece puzzle. "You have done 3. How many more?" (3.)

TOPIC 0.16 — MAZES AND FOLLOWING SIMPLE DIRECTIONS (spatial reasoning)
================================================================================

Q1 (DESCRIPTIVE)
SET-UP : Make a simple path on the floor with tape or cushions, from a
         "start" (a toy dog) to a "home" (a basket). Hide a treat in the
         basket.
ASK    : "Help the dog get home! Walk forward. Now turn. Go around the
          chair. Go under the table. Where did you find the treat?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  Follows the path and the spoken directions. Says "In the basket!" or
  "At home!"
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. "A PATH is the way you go from one place to another."
  2. Give one instruction at a time: "Walk forward", "Stop", "Turn",
     "Go around", "Go under", "Go over".
  3. Point out that some ways are blocked: "Can we go through the wall?
     No! We must go around."
  4. On paper later: draw a very wide, simple maze with a crayon — from a
     rabbit to a carrot — with only one turn.
  Why it matters: this builds spatial thinking — the ability to picture
  where things are and how to move between them (the root of geometry,
  maps and directions).
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  A straight path with no turns; walk it together holding hands.
TRY NEXT:
  Give two directions at once: "Go around the chair AND then under the
  table."

Q2 (NUMERICAL)
SET-UP : A path of 5 cushions (or 5 paper "stepping stones") in a line.
ASK    : "Jump on the stones and count your jumps. How many jumps to reach
          the end? If you go back 2 jumps, which stone are you on?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  - 5 jumps to reach the end.
  - Going back 2 jumps: on stone number 3.
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Count each jump aloud as the child lands: "1, 2, 3, 4, 5! You made it!"
  2. "Now jump back two. One... two." The child is on the 3rd stone.
  3. Count from the start to check: "1, 2, 3 — yes, stone 3."
  This path is a real-life NUMBER LINE: moving forward adds, moving back
  takes away.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Only 3 stones; go back 1 jump (stone 2).
TRY NEXT:
  "Start on stone 1, jump forward 2. Where are you?" (Stone 3.)

TOPIC 0.17 — EVERYDAY MATHS TALK: counting stairs, grouping toys, noticing
             numbers around
================================================================================

Q1 (DESCRIPTIVE)
SET-UP : A walk around the house or the street.
ASK    : "Let's be number detectives! Where can you see a number? What is
          it for?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  Points to numbers on the clock, a door, a lift button, a phone, a car's
  number plate, a birthday card. May say "Clock!", "My age!", "Lift!"
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. "Numbers are everywhere! They help us every day."
  2. Explain simply what each number does:
       - Clock numbers tell us the TIME.
       - House numbers tell us WHERE someone lives.
       - Lift buttons tell us which FLOOR.
       - Price tags tell us how much money something COSTS.
       - Your age tells how many BIRTHDAYS you have had.
  3. Talk maths all day long: "Let's count the stairs", "Give me 2
     spoons", "Which banana is longer?", "Is your cup full?"
  Everyday maths talk is the most powerful teaching tool at this age —
  children learn maths the way they learn language: by hearing it often
  in real life.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Point out just one number each day, e.g. the child's age on a cake.
TRY NEXT:
  "Find the number 2 on the clock / remote / lift." (Recognising numerals
  begins in Stage 1.)

Q2 (NUMERICAL)
SET-UP : A short staircase (or 5 steps), and a pile of toys at the bottom.
ASK    : "Let's count the steps as we climb. How many steps? How old are
          you? Show me with your fingers. Let's give 2 toys to teddy and
          1 toy to dolly. How many toys did we give away?"
EXPECTED ANSWER:
  - Counts the steps correctly (e.g. 5 steps).
  - Shows age on fingers (e.g. 2 or 3 fingers).
  - Toys given away: 2 + 1 = 3.
TEACHER'S EXPLANATION:
  1. Stairs: say one number for each step as the foot lands — "1, 2, 3,
     4, 5." One number for one step (one-to-one correspondence from Topic
     0.10).
  2. Age: "You are 2. Show 2 fingers. Show me how old you will be on your
     next birthday!" (One more finger = 3.)
  3. Toys: give 2 to teddy ("one, two") and 1 to dolly ("one"). Then
     count all the toys given away: "1, 2, 3." "We gave away 3 toys."
  These everyday questions join together everything from Stage 0:
  counting, matching, more/less and position.
IF THE CHILD STRUGGLES:
  Count just 3 steps. Give 1 toy to teddy and 1 to dolly (total 2).
TRY NEXT:
  "There are 4 apples. We eat 1. How many are left?" (3.)

END OF STAGE 0 — READINESS CHECKLIST FOR STAGE 1
================================================================================
The child is ready for Stage 1 (Nursery / Pre-K) when they can usually:
  [ ] Match identical objects and say "same" / "different"
  [ ] Sort a mixed group by one property (colour, size, shape or type)
  [ ] Use big/small, long/short, heavy/light, full/empty correctly
  [ ] Tell which group has more when the difference is easy to see
  [ ] Follow position words: in, on, under, beside, front, behind
  [ ] Say the counting words 1 to 10 in order (at least 1 to 5 reliably)
  [ ] Recognise 1, 2 and 3 objects without counting
  [ ] Give "one for each" (one spoon per doll)
  [ ] Name a circle, square and triangle
  [ ] Continue a simple AB pattern (red-blue-red-blue)
  [ ] Stack or nest 3-4 items by size
  [ ] Use first / next / last for daily routines
  [ ] Complete a 2-4 piece puzzle and a shape sorter
  [ ] Follow a simple path with 1-2 directions
  [ ] Enjoy talking about numbers and counting in daily life

Conservation of number (Topic 0.9) is NOT required to move on — most
children understand it fully only between ages 4 and 7. Keep revisiting it.
